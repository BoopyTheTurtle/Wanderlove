# Community listening: feeling disconnected

Roadmap deliverable A2. People who write publicly about drifting from a partner return to a small set of complaints:
the relationship has shrunk to logistics, phones and tiredness eat the evening, effort goes unnoticed, and small
attempts to connect get missed. This document records those complaints as themes, says roughly how often each
appeared, and turns them into task, reminder, and feature ideas for Wannadoo.

## 1. Method and limits

**What I read.** Over one session I ran about 35 web searches and read pages one at a time, as a person would. I read
13 public threads in full and saw about 45 more through search results, where the title and summary showed the
complaint but not every reply. I also read eight published surveys and trend reports and about twenty articles by
therapists and journalists that summarise reader letters and client patterns.

| Source type                       | Where                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Parenting and family forums (UK)  | Mumsnet (Relationships, Parenting, AIBU, Stay-at-home parents, Chat), Gransnet                                                              |
| General relationship forums       | LoveShack (US), Talk About Marriage (US, titles only), WeddingWire forums (US), MoneySavingExpert forums (UK), Beyond Blue forums (AU, titles only) |
| Long-distance forum               | Loving From A Distance                                                                                                                      |
| Non-English and non-US            | urbia.de (Germany), German counselling blogs on _nebeneinander herleben_, Indian counselling blogs and Quora titles on joint-family life   |
| Q&A and confessions               | Quora, GirlsAskGuys, Glassdoor Community (titles and summaries only)                                                                        |
| Surveys and reports               | Pew Research Center (2020, phone snubbing), Talker Research for Lovense (2025, relationship ruts), Relate and eharmony _The Way We Are Now_ (2021), Greene King "Shoulder to Shoulder" poll (UK), app-store and press reviews of the Paired app |

**How I counted.** A theme's frequency is the number of distinct threads, reviews, or survey items in which I saw it,
not the number of people. "Read" counts threads I opened; "seen" adds those I met only through a search summary. Treat
every number as a rough rank, not a measurement.

**What I stored.** Themes only, paraphrased. No usernames, no quotes, no links to individual posts. The source list at
the end names forums and published surveys, never threads.

**What blocked me.** Reddit refused automated page reads, so this document contains no Reddit threads read in full;
Reddit appears only where search summaries or articles report its discussions. Beyond Blue, Quora, GirlsAskGuys, and
Glassdoor returned "forbidden", and Talk About Marriage routes readers through a paid-access gateway, so I skipped
them past their titles. Reddit skews young, male, and American; its absence tilts this sample the other way.

**Biases to keep in mind.**

- Forums collect trouble. Happy couples rarely post, so the sample overstates distress and understates what works.
- Mumsnet dominates the fully read threads, so mothers of young children in the UK speak loudest. Men's views come
  mostly through surveys, review sites, and thread titles.
- Forum replies escalate. Many threads end with "see a counsellor" or "leave him"; that reflects forum culture more
  than the poster's situation.
- Some threads that open as "we've drifted" turn out to describe control or cruelty. Wannadoo cannot fix those, and
  its tasks must not pressure anyone inside one.
- Survey figures come from press releases, some commissioned by companies with products to sell. I cite them only
  as direction.

## 2. Themes

Each theme lists what people describe, how often I met it (read / seen), who it seems to affect, and what it suggests
for Wannadoo.

### Routine and boredom

**R1. Roommates, not partners.** Couples share a home, a calendar, and often children, yet feel like flatmates: each
does their own thing, evenings pass side by side, and nobody can say when it changed. Germans call it living
_nebeneinander her_; British posters say housemates. Frequency: 5 read / 15 seen, plus nearly every therapist article.
Affects: long-term couples of every age, parents most. Suggests: the core pitch. A quest is time spent facing each
other rather than the telly, and the wrap-up can name one thing the couple did _together_ today.

**R2. The evening rut.** The same dinner, the same show, the same scroll, every night and weekend. One 2025 US survey
put a quarter of partnered adults in a rut now and a third in one before, lasting ten months on average. Frequency:
2 read / 7 seen, 1 survey. Affects: couples from about six months in, busy workers. Suggests: a reminder that offers a
short walk as the swap for tonight's episode, not an addition to it.

**R3. We stopped doing our things.** Couples who once hiked, went to films, or cooked together dropped it after a
baby, a job, or a move, and miss it more than they miss grand dates. Frequency: 2 read / 6 seen. Affects: parents and
couples after a life change. Suggests: a deep task that asks "what did we used to do that we should bring back?" and
saves the answer as a seed for a later quest or reminder.

**R4. The honeymoon ends.** Newer couples notice fewer texts, less affection, and eye-rolls where there used to be
delight, and ask whether the drop means doom. Replies split between "normal, now build something" and "get out".
Frequency: 1 read / 9 seen. Affects: couples between three months and two years. Suggests: tasks that build new shared
references (inside jokes, firsts) instead of probing commitment; the tone must stay light for this group.

### Phones and attention

**P1. Side by side, on separate screens.** The picture recurs: both partners in bed or on the sofa, each scrolling.
The phone lets people withdraw without either admitting it. About half of US partnered adults say their partner has
been distracted by a phone while they talked (Pew, 2020). Frequency: 0 read / 6 seen, 3 surveys or studies. Affects:
everyone, younger couples and parents of small children most. Suggests: Wannadoo itself must pull the phone away, not
toward. Screens should appear only to show the next task, then ask to be pocketed.

**P2. Missed small bids.** A partner points out a bird, a funny sign, or a thought, and the other ignores it. The 2025
TikTok "bird test" turned Gottman's idea of bids for connection into a viral check, and people argued about it
widely. Frequency: 0 read / 5 seen, plus the trend coverage. Affects: all. Suggests: a silly game in which each
partner must "bid" on something they see en route and the other must turn toward it; a walk is full of birds.

### Feeling unseen

**U1. My effort has become invisible.** What someone does every day (the school run, the admin, the cooking, the
earning) stops being thanked and starts being expected. Men voice this most on review and advice sites; women voice
it as the mental load. Frequency: 3 read / 8 seen. Affects: long-term couples, parents. Suggests: a wrap-up task
"thank them for one thing nobody ever thanks them for", and a reminder that asks one partner to notice one unthanked
job before the next quest.

**U2. I'm only a parent now.** After a baby, people feel reduced to a function: mum, dad, earner. They want to be seen
as the person they were before. Frequency: 3 read / 8 seen. Affects: new parents, especially mothers. Suggests: an
introductory task that bans talk of the children for the first stop, and questions about the partner as a person
("what did you want to be at fifteen?").

**U3. No compliments, no sense of being wanted.** People cannot remember the last time their partner praised their
looks, character, or effort. When a Mumsnet thread asked what made posters feel good about themselves, praise and
visible attraction led the answers. Frequency: 2 read / 6 seen. Affects: all; men report it with surprise, as rare
enough to remember. Suggests: a silly game of specific compliments (one about each stop's theme), and a wrap-up that
asks each to name something they found attractive today.

### Time and logistics

**T1. We only talk about logistics.** Conversation shrinks to shopping, pick-ups, bills, and who does what. Nearly
every therapist article names this as the first sign of drift. Frequency: 3 read / 10 seen. Affects: working couples,
parents. Suggests: a rule, stated at the start of each quest, that logistics wait until home; and deep-task questions
that cannot be answered with a plan.

**T2. Long hours and opposite shifts.** One partner works nights, late, or away; the other feels like a single parent
or a lone flatmate. Replies recommend one protected shared day, notes left for each other, and messages on breaks.
Frequency: 3 read / 6 seen. Affects: shift workers, high-hours jobs, stay-at-home parents. Suggests: a reminder keyed
to the couple's shared free window, and a short "one-stop" quest for weeks with little time.

**T3. Nothing left by evening.** Both are drained; the relationship gets whatever energy remains, which is none.
Parents compete over who is more tired. Frequency: 3 read / 7 seen. Affects: parents, both-working couples, Indian
urban couples with long commutes. Suggests: quests must cost little: a short loop, no preparation, tasks that give
energy (laughing, moving) before they ask for depth.

**T4. No privacy at home.** Couples living with parents or in-laws, or in small flats with children, cannot talk
freely or touch without an audience. Frequency: 0 read / 4 seen (mostly Indian sources). Affects: joint-family
households, young couples living with parents. Suggests: the walk itself is the feature. Market it as "the one place
you two are alone together".

### Communication

**C1. One partner barely talks.** A quiet partner shrugs at home yet sparkles with friends; the other feels lonely
beside them. Replies split between "introvert, safe at home" and "that would end it for me". Frequency: 2 read / 5
seen. Affects: long-term couples; often framed as a gender pattern. Suggests: tasks that give the quiet partner a
structure (choose, rank, point) rather than an open "tell me about your feelings".

**C2. We have run out of things to say.** Long-distance couples on daily calls, empty-nesters at a silent dinner, and
couples a year in all report the well running dry. The fix people recommend: do something together and talk about
that. Frequency: 3 read / 7 seen. Affects: long-distance couples, empty-nesters, couples in year one or two. Suggests:
each stop gives the couple a fresh object to talk about (a building, a view, a stranger's dog), which is Wannadoo's
edge over question-card apps.

**C3. Say what you need.** Replies across forums tell posters to state needs directly instead of hoping their partner
guesses; posters say that feels risky or pointless. Frequency: 6 read / 8 seen, mostly in replies. Affects: all.
Suggests: a low-stakes deep-task format such as "finish the sentence: I feel close to you when…", which states a need
as a positive.

**C4. Side by side is easier.** In a UK poll, about half of men said they discuss personal worries more easily side by
side than face to face, as on a walk or a drive. Frequency: 1 survey, 4 articles. Affects: men especially, and anyone
who dislikes eye contact during hard talk. Suggests: put the deep task mid-walk, while moving, not at a stop facing
each other.

### Affection and appreciation

**A1. Small unasked gestures.** When a thread asked happy couples what their partner does unprompted, tea or coffee
in bed led by far (15+ replies), then food, listening, touch, and chores taken over without being asked. Frequency:
2 read / 4 seen. Affects: all. Suggests: the wrap-up's "small thing" task, and reminders between quests that propose a
concrete gesture ("make them a drink before they ask").

**A2. Touch fades first.** Hand-holding, hugs, and casual touch go before sex does. New parents report being
touched-out, which makes the other partner feel rejected. Frequency: 3 read / 6 seen. Affects: parents of infants,
long-term couples. Suggests: a light, optional touch task (walk a stretch holding hands) that never escalates and
reads as sweet in public.

**A3. Humour repairs.** After an argument, an inside joke or a silly message signals "we're fine" faster than a talk.
Frequency: 2 read / 3 seen. Affects: all, long-distance couples notably. Suggests: silly games that leave behind a
private joke, and a way to replay one later ("remember the pigeon?").

### Conflict

**F1. The same fight, again.** Couples argue about dishes, lunches, and forms, and the fight returns days later
because it concerns fairness, not the dishes. Frequency: 3 read / 6 seen. Affects: both-working couples and parents.
Suggests: Wannadoo should stay clear of chores as a topic; a deep task can instead ask "what do I do that helps you
most?", which surfaces fairness gently.

**F2. Keeping score.** Tiredness contests and mental tallies of who did what poison goodwill. Happy posters describe
being on the same team. Frequency: 3 read / 3 seen. Affects: parents, busy couples. Suggests: cooperative games only;
never pit the partners' scores against each other.

**F3. Criticism and put-downs.** Constant correction framed as help, and jokes at a partner's expense, erode
confidence. Frequency: 2 read / 3 seen. Affects: all. Suggests: silly games must never make one partner the butt of
the joke; teasing tasks need Edgar's close review.

### Life stages

**L1. New baby.** Exhaustion, touched-out bodies, unequal load, and conversations only about the baby. Veterans
promise it improves after the first year, and name adult-only time as the help. Frequency: 3 read / 8 seen. Affects:
parents of under-threes. Suggests: a pram-friendly quest (flat loop, benches) and a first-stop rule of no baby talk.

**L2. Long distance.** Connected yet disconnected: calls grow thin, post-visit blues bite, and a shared end date keeps
couples going. Frequency: 2 read / 8 seen. Affects: long-distance couples. Suggests: a visit quest that marks the
reunion, and a question worth deciding: should partners in two cities walk "together" at the same time?

**L3. Empty nest and retirement.** Kids leave or work ends, and couples find they ran a household well but no longer
know each other. Retirement also brings too much togetherness, and people ask for protected time apart. Frequency:
1 read / 6 seen. Affects: couples over fifty. Suggests: "rediscover" deep tasks about who each has become, and a pace
that suits slower walkers.

**L4. Different needs for closeness.** One wants shared evenings and outings; the other wants long solitude and calls
that normal. Replies urge both hobbies apart and a protected shared slot. Frequency: 3 read / 4 seen. Affects: all
ages. Suggests: Wannadoo should respect solitude. Frame a quest as a bounded hour, not a demand for more togetherness.

## 3. What people think of relationship apps, date ideas, and structured activities

These opinions set Wannadoo's tone.

**What people find cringe or off-putting**

- **Homework.** A daily question becomes a chore within weeks; reviewers of the Paired app say so most often.
- **One-sided use.** The keen partner writes paragraphs; the other answers in one line or stops opening the app. Some
  partners dismiss such apps as nonsense before trying them.
- **Clinical prompts.** Therapy-flavoured questions ("what stops you opening up?") feel like an exam and put all the
  work on the answerer.
- **Too deep too soon.** New couples meet questions about money, marriage, and children they are not ready for.
- **Repetition.** Long-term users notice recycled questions.
- **Forced date nights.** Scheduled, fancy, or compulsory dates feel awkward and kill spontaneity; therapists compare
  them to making a child say sorry.
- **Public or gamified intimacy.** Some users call popular couples apps too public, too gamified, or too one-sided.
- **Staring exercises.** The 36-questions experiment's four minutes of eye contact feels awkward for the first
  half-minute; some find the whole list too intense for a new relationship.

**What people welcome**

- **Doing, not only talking.** Long-distance and bored couples alike recommend shared activities that give them
  something to talk about.
- **Novelty.** Couples in a rut want a new place or a new thing, not a better version of the same evening.
- **Small, concrete gestures.** A cup of tea brought unasked outranks grand romance in every thread about feeling loved.
- **Laughter.** Inside jokes and silliness repair and bond.
- **Low effort, flexible, skippable.** Tired couples want something that fits a spare half-hour and forgives a missed
  week.
- **Something to look forward to.** A midweek walk or a planned visit gives the week a bright spot.
- **Questions that lead somewhere.** The 36 questions work for many once the first awkward minutes pass; people
  treasure the one surprising thing they learn.

**Tone implications for Wannadoo**

- Sound like a playful friend, never a therapist or a coach.
- Make every task skippable without comment, as the roadmap already requires.
- Let the walk carry the depth: a question asked while moving feels lighter than one asked across a table.
- Design for the less keen partner. If the reluctant one enjoys the first quest, the keen one needs no persuading.
- Keep quests occasional and bounded; never make streaks or daily obligations the point.

## 4. The ten themes most useful for Wannadoo

Ranked by how often they appeared, how directly a walking quest answers them, and how safely an app can touch them.

| 1    | R1 Roommates, not partners              | The most common complaint across every forum and country; a quest is the direct antidote to parallel evenings               |
| 2    | T1 We only talk about logistics         | Near-universal first sign of drift; a "no logistics on the walk" rule costs nothing and changes the conversation            |
| 3    | P1 Side by side, on separate screens    | Widely shared and measurable; Wannadoo can prove itself by getting phones into pockets                                      |
| 4    | C2 We have run out of things to say     | Each stop supplies a fresh topic, which question-card apps cannot                                                          |
| 5    | P2 Missed small bids                    | Culturally current thanks to the bird test, and a walk supplies bids at every corner; a natural silly game                  |
| 6    | U1 My effort has become invisible       | Frequent among both men and women; appreciation tasks are cheap, safe, and felt at once                                     |
| 7    | A1 Small unasked gestures               | The clearest picture of what feeling loved looks like; feeds reminders between quests                                      |
| 8    | R2 The evening rut                      | A quest is novelty on the doorstep; reminders can offer the swap at the moment the rut starts                              |
| 9    | C4 Side by side is easier               | Explains why a walk beats a dinner for deep talk, especially for men; shapes where the deep task sits                      |

Close behind: U2 (only a parent now) and A3 (humour repairs). The conflict themes matter for tone but make poor task
material; Wannadoo should stay out of chore fights.

## 5. Ideas

The walk order is introductory, silly game, deep task, silly game, wrap-up. Ideas below name the theme they answer.
All must work in public, on a short loop, without props.

### Introductory

1. **Pocket pact.** Both phones go on silent; one person holds both until the next stop. (P1)
2. **No-logistics toll.** Anyone who mentions shopping, bills, or schedules before stop three owes the other a
   compliment. (T1)
3. **Before us.** Tell one thing about yourself at fifteen your partner has never heard. (U2, R1)
4. **Today in one word.** Each gives a word for their day; the other guesses why. (T1)
5. **Rewind.** Name the first walk or outing you two remember and one detail from it. (R3, R4)
6. **Weather report.** Each rates their energy from drizzle to sunshine, so the other knows how to pitch the walk. (T3)

### Silly games

1. **Bird spotting.** Each must point out three small things on the way; the other must stop and react with full
   enthusiasm. (P2)
2. **Street compliments.** Pick an object at this stop; compliment your partner using it as a metaphor. (U3)
3. **Narrator.** One narrates the other's walk to the next stop like a nature documentary. (A3, C1)
4. **Inside-joke factory.** Invent a name for a thing you pass; use it for the rest of the walk. (A3, R4)
5. **Would you rather, local edition.** Two choices drawn from what you can see right now. (C2)
6. **Same answer.** On three, both point at the best house, tree, or window here; score together, not against each
   other. (F2)
7. **Hand-holding relay.** Hold hands until you pass something red; then swap sides. (A2)

### Deep tasks (asked while walking)

1. **Bring it back.** What did we used to do together that we should do again? The answer becomes a seed for a
   future reminder. (R3)
2. **Close when.** Finish the sentence: I feel close to you when… (C3, R1)
3. **Unseen work.** What do I do that you think nobody notices? Then trade. (U1)
4. **Who you've become.** What has changed in you this year that I might have missed? (L3, U2)
5. **Help that counts.** What small thing that I do helps you most? (F1, A1)
6. **Look forward.** What would you like us to have done together a year from now? (L2, R2)
7. **Choose, don't explain.** Rank three memories of us from best to funniest; talk only if you want to. (C1)

### Wrap-up

1. **Thank the invisible.** Thank your partner for one thing nobody ever thanks them for. (U1)
2. **Attractive today.** Name one moment on this walk when you found them attractive. (U3)
3. **Together today.** Name the one thing you two did today that you would not have done on the sofa. (R1, R2)
4. **Small promise.** Each picks one small gesture to do for the other before the next quest. (A1)
5. **Photo of us.** One picture at the last stop, both in frame, no phones in hand. (P1)

### Reminders and notifications

Keep them rare, specific, and kind; a reminder should suggest one act, never scold.

- **Swap, not add.** At the couple's usual free evening: "Twenty minutes before the episode? A short loop is ready."
  (R2, T3)
- **Seeded return.** "Last walk you said you missed [answer]. Fancy it this weekend?" from the "bring it back" answer.
  (R3)
- **Promise nudge.** A day after the quest: a private reminder of the small gesture each chose. (A1)
- **Notice one thing.** Midweek: "Spot one job your partner did today that nobody thanked them for." (U1)
- **Tea before asked.** "Make them a drink before they ask tonight." (A1)
- **Bird of the day.** "Send your partner one small thing you saw today, nothing practical." (P2, T2)
- **Shift-friendly slot.** For couples with little overlap: a one-stop quest offered at their shared free window. (T2)
- **Visit quest.** For long-distance couples, a reunion quest unlocked when both phones are in one city. (L2)
- **Gentle return after a gap.** After weeks without a quest: "No streak to lose. Ready when you both are." (Section 3)
