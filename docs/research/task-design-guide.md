# Task design guide

Status: draft for Edgar's review, September 30, 2026. Deliverable A1 of the [MVP roadmap](../mvp-roadmap.md); feeds
stage 3 (the task data model) and stage 4 (the task pool).

Every quest has five stops and one task at each, in a fixed arc: **1 introductory, 2 silly game, 3 deep task, 4 silly
game, 5 wrap-up**. Each stop also takes a photo or a skip. This guide sets the rules for writing those tasks: what the
evidence supports, how each category works, what the interviews add, a template that maps to code, 40 worked
examples, and a checklist for reviewing the pool.

## 1. Principles

### What the evidence supports

The [closeness research](Closeness_research.md) and its critique leave four ingredients standing. Tasks lean on these
and on nothing weaker.

| Ingredient                                | Source                                            | Strength                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Novelty and mild challenge, done together | Aron's self-expansion studies (1993, 2000)        | The most experimentally tested; lab effects measured short-term, one 10-week field study                               |
| Genuine enthusiasm for good news          | Gable et al. (2004)                               | Undebunked but mostly correlational and self-report; works only when the enthusiasm is real                            |
| Perceived responsiveness and appreciation | Joel et al. (2020, 43 datasets); Birnbaum and Reis | The common thread across large pooled data; describes happy couples, cannot promise a couple becomes happier           |
| Reciprocal, gently escalating disclosure  | Aron et al. (1997); social penetration theory     | Tested on strangers, not couples; the format (turns, both answer, start light) transfers better than the exact questions |

Three popular ideas fail as foundations. Gottman's headline numbers lack independent replication; the synchrony effect
may be placebo; the "people want deeper talk" finding comes from strangers. A task may still borrow their form, such as
mirroring movements, but only because the game is fun, never because it claims an effect.

The critique's plainest lesson shapes every task: a question works when it reflects listening, not as a trick. Tasks
therefore build in a follow-up question or a repeat-back, so the listener shows they heard.

### Limits

Every task stays inside these limits. The review checklist (section 6) repeats them.

- **Invite, never diagnose.** Tasks make no therapeutic claim and never score or judge the relationship.
- **Skip is always allowed,** without comment, penalty, or a sad face. Skipping a task still counts the stop.
- **No conflict topics in public.** Money, chores, sex, family tensions, exes, and past fights stay out. Deep tasks
  touch "are you there for me?" through appreciation and wishes, never through grievances.
- **Strangers stay out of it.** No task mocks, films, follows, or involves a stranger. Asking a passer-by anything is
  out of the pool for now.
- **Nothing unsafe.** No task happens in or near the road, at the water's edge, on anything to climb, or with eyes
  closed while walking. Movement games say "find a safe, open spot first".
- **Accessible where possible.** Most tasks work sitting down or standing still. A task that needs walking, jumping,
  or running carries the `move` tag, so the app can offer another.
- **Phone only.** No purchases, props, pens, or food. Things found at the stop (a leaf, a view) are fine to look at;
  nothing gets picked, broken, or taken.
- **Plain English.** Many users speak English as a second language. Short sentences, common words, no idioms.
- **Both play.** Every task gives both partners a turn. Nobody only watches.

## 2. The four categories

The arc climbs and lands. The opener warms, the first game loosens, the deep task opens, the second game releases,
and the wrap-up closes. The second stop's laughter buys the third stop's openness.

| Stop | Category     | Time at stop | Energy    | Depth         |
| ---- | ------------ | ------------ | --------- | ------------- |
| 1    | Introductory | 2–4 min      | Calm      | Light         |
| 2, 4 | Silly game   | 3–5 min      | High      | Light         |
| 3    | Deep task    | 4–7 min      | Calm      | Deepest point |
| 5    | Wrap-up      | 2–4 min      | Calm-warm | Medium        |

A full quest spends about 15–25 minutes on tasks, next to roughly 25–30 minutes of walking.

### Introductory (stop 1)

- **Purpose.** Set the tone for the route: cute, curious, "we are on a little adventure together".
- **Mechanism.** Reciprocal, light disclosure (Aron 1997, social penetration theory). Evidence: moderate for the
  format, drawn from strangers; safe to apply at the light end.
- **Tone.** Playful and warm. Questions that invite a small story, not a fact or a rating.
- **Length.** 2–4 minutes.
- **Format.** Both answer the same prompt. One goes first; the app can suggest who ("the taller one starts"). The
  listener asks one follow-up before swapping.
- **Avoid.** Anything heavy, anything about the relationship's problems, quiz questions with a right answer that
  one partner might fail ("what's my favourite colour?").

### Silly game (stops 2 and 4)

- **Purpose.** Pull both partners slightly out of their comfort zone, as a team, so they feel like an in-group: us
  two, a little ridiculous, together.
- **Mechanism.** Novelty and mild challenge shared as a pair (Aron 2000). Evidence: the strongest of the four,
  though measured over short periods. Team framing matters: the pair plays with each other against the task, never
  against each other in a way that humiliates.
- **Tone.** Silly, generous, quick. Laughing at the game, never at the partner.
- **Length.** 3–5 minutes.
- **Format.** A clear goal, a short time limit, and roles that swap. Performance games happen for the partner, not
  for the street. Every game has a "small version": whispering instead of singing, a tiny pose instead of a big one.
- **Avoid.** Strangers as audience or target; anything that could look like harassment or filming people; running
  near traffic; games that need a winner and a loser without a rematch; anything a shy person cannot do quietly.

Stops 2 and 4 draw from the same pool, so silly games run out twice as fast as the other categories. Stage 4 should
aim for the top of the 30–50 range here.

### Deep task (stop 3)

- **Purpose.** A sweet, caring moment to reflect on the relationship: each partner feels seen and valued.
- **Mechanism.** Perceived responsiveness and appreciation (Joel et al. 2020; Birnbaum and Reis), and active-
  constructive response to good news (Gable 2004). Evidence: the common thread of the field, but correlational; the
  task creates the moment and cannot promise the outcome.
- **Tone.** Tender, specific, unhurried. Positive form: "something you'd love more of", never "something that
  bothers you".
- **Length.** 4–7 minutes. The longest stop; the app should suggest sitting down if a bench is near.
- **Format.** Both answer, in turns. The listener responds before swapping: a follow-up question, a repeat-back
  ("so what I hear is…"), or a thank-you. Tasks ask for a specific moment or example, not a general statement.
- **Avoid.** Grievances, "what should I change", fears, regrets, childhood wounds, and anything that needs a
  therapist in the room. Tier 3–4 questions from the date-activities list ("a wound from your past") belong in no
  public walk.

### Wrap-up (stop 5)

- **Purpose.** A feeling of completion: the walk was ours, and it went well.
- **Mechanism.** Active-constructive reflection (Gable) turned on the walk itself, plus a small appreciation. Evidence:
  as for Gable; low risk.
- **Tone.** Light, warm, a little proud. Ends on something that went right.
- **Length.** 2–4 minutes.
- **Format.** Both answer. The prompt looks back at this walk, then optionally one small step forward (a plan for
  this week). A good wrap-up ends in a natural photo.
- **Avoid.** Rating the partner or the relationship; homework; anything that depends on an earlier task's answer,
  since tasks must stand alone.

## 3. What the interviews and date activities add

Five interviews (men from their 30s to their 70s, together from six months to twenty years) and a synthesis of
question decks and date-activity sources point the same way as the research. They add six needs.

- **Doing beats sitting.** Several interviewees prefer active dates because cinema is silent and dinner makes talk
  hard; they want to see the partner "in action". One noted that the more active the activity, the less room for
  anxious thoughts. Silly games carry this.
- **Ideas are the pain point.** Coming up with something fresh is a hassle, and the longer a couple is together, the
  more dates repeat. One asked outright for something that throws out random fun things, such as secret-agent games.
  The no-repeat rule and a large pool answer this.
- **Novelty and a small "wow".** Several stressed trying something new and a moment outside the usual routine, even
  on an ordinary day. Silly games should feel surprising, not like a worksheet.
- **Relax, don't perform.** One partner turns goal-oriented and forgets to enjoy the moment; a reminder that nothing
  needs to go perfectly helps. Tasks avoid scores, timers that punish, and pressure to finish.
- **Personal questions are hard; leading ones backfire.** One interviewee named personal questions as the difficult
  part and warned against overly leading prompts. Deep tasks ask open questions and leave the answer to the couple.
- **Free and phone-only.** Budget matters, especially for students, and nobody reported paying for date tools.
  Tasks cost nothing.

Three smaller points shape details. Photos get taken and then forgotten, so each task suggests a photo worth keeping.
Getting through a tricky situation together (an escape room) felt bonding, which supports team framing in games.
Chemistry lives in looks and body language as much as talk, so some tasks use faces, poses, and movement, not only
words.

The competitor notes (Let's Roam and similar) add two warnings: users dislike confusing or poorly placed challenges,
and they want to keep their photos. Every task must be clear on first reading.

The date-activities synthesis confirms the format rules: turns rather than interviews, start easy and escalate,
ask for a story instead of a fact, and build in a follow-up. It also notes that familiar, comfortable time predicts
daily satisfaction as well as novelty does, which supports the calm stops between the games.

## 4. Task template

Each task is one record. The fields map directly to a TypeScript type in `packages/core`.

| Field       | Type       | Rule                                                                                        |
| ----------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `id`        | string     | `<category>-<nnn>`, e.g. `silly-014`; never reused or renumbered, since history keys on it |
| `category`  | enum       | `intro`, `silly`, `deep`, or `wrapup`                                                       |
| `title`     | string     | 2–5 words, shown as the stop's heading                                                      |
| `prompt`    | string     | The text shown to the couple; at most 40 words, second person plural                        |
| `steps`     | string[]   | How to do it: 2–4 short steps, each one action                                              |
| `photoHint` | string     | A suggested photo for this stop; one sentence                                               |
| `tags`      | TaskTag[]  | From the fixed list below                                                                   |
| `minutes`   | number     | Estimated time at the stop, within the category's range                                     |

Tags:

| Tag             | Meaning                                                              |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `talk`          | Mostly conversation                                                  |
| `move`          | Needs walking, stepping, or arm movement; offer another for low mobility |
| `voice`         | Speaking up, singing, or acting out loud                             |
| `quiet`         | Works in a whisper; fine near other people                           |
| `energetic`     | High energy; best early in the day or when both feel lively          |
| `outdoors-only` | Needs sky, plants, or open space                                     |
| `seated-ok`     | Works fully while sitting on a bench                                 |

```ts
export type TaskCategory = 'intro' | 'silly' | 'deep' | 'wrapup';
export type TaskTag = 'talk' | 'move' | 'voice' | 'quiet' | 'energetic' | 'outdoors-only' | 'seated-ok';

export interface QuestTask {
  id: string;
  category: TaskCategory;
  title: string;
  prompt: string;
  steps: string[];
  photoHint: string;
  tags: TaskTag[];
  minutes: number;
}
```

Place-aware tasks (roadmap section 5) can later add an optional `placeTags` field without changing the rest.

## 6. Review checklist

Apply every line to every task. A single "no" sends the task back for rewriting.

**Content**

- [ ] The task fits its category's purpose and sits within its time range.
- [ ] Both partners take a turn, and the listener responds (follow-up, repeat-back, or thanks) where the task is a
      question.
- [ ] Deep tasks ask for a specific moment or example, in positive form.
- [ ] The task stands alone; it depends on no other task's answer.
- [ ] The task claims no effect and makes no promise about the relationship.

**Safety and consent**

- [ ] No conflict topics: money, chores, sex, family tensions, exes, or past fights.
- [ ] No strangers as audience, target, or participant; no filming of other people.
- [ ] Nothing happens in or near the road, water, heights, or with eyes closed while moving.
- [ ] A shy person can do a quiet version; nobody is forced to perform loudly.
- [ ] Nothing gets picked, broken, taken, or left behind.

**Access**

- [ ] The task works standing still or sitting, or it carries the `move` tag.
- [ ] It needs only a phone: no purchase, prop, pen, or food.
- [ ] It works in town and in the country, or it carries `outdoors-only`.

**Writing**

- [ ] The prompt has at most 40 words and speaks to "you two" in the second person plural.
- [ ] A non-native speaker understands it on first reading: short sentences, common words, no idioms.
- [ ] The tone matches the category: warm, playful, never mocking.
- [ ] The photo hint suggests a photo worth keeping.
- [ ] The id follows `<category>-<nnn>` and is new.

**In the field**

- [ ] Edgar or a test couple has tried it at a real stop, and it felt fine in public.
